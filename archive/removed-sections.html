The question

When does a free automated fact-checker help a community learn the truth?

Social platforms increasingly build AI assistants into their interfaces, and many users already consult them to check claims. Such tools are quick and easy, but imperfect. When someone consults one privately, others see only what she does, not what the tool told her. So a mistaken check can spread through actions and shape what a whole community comes to believe.

+ Broader checking

The tool lets more agents check

Manual verification takes time and effort, so only the most undecided agents bother. A free tool is worth using for a much wider range of beliefs. A truthful article that everyone would otherwise dismiss unchecked now gets verified by some, and can escape a wrong cascade.

− Misleading errors

The tool’s mistakes become other people’s evidence

With perfect manual checking, a crowd that nobody tricks into the wrong action always reaches the right conclusion. Automated fact-checking errs, and its errors are passed on through the actions that follow, starting wrong cascades that manual checking would have avoided.

The paper proves which effect wins, where, and by how much accuracy has to improve before the tool pays off. Everything below runs live in your browser using the same model and Monte Carlo procedure as the paper.

The model

Agents arrive one by one, and only actions are public

  1. An article appears

    It has an observable reliability r: the prior probability that it is truthful. A truthful article carries the true state with probability p > ½; a fake one is uninformative.

  2. Agents arrive in turn

    Agent t has her own prior belief bt (think ideology) and sees every earlier agent’s action: like or dislike. She does not see their private checks.

  3. She may check first

    A check returns a private verdict: verified or falsified. She checks when the value of that information exceeds its cost.

  4. She acts

    She likes what she believes true and dislikes what she believes fake, and earns 1 if she is right. Welfare is the probability that the crowd ends in a correct cascade.

Manual fact-checking

Costs KH in time and effort, but is error-free. An idealised, clean baseline.

Automated fact-checking

Free, but wrong sometimes: it calls a truthful article fake with probability e1 and passes a fake one with probability e2. Tools are typically better at spotting falsehoods, so e1 > e2.

What is an information cascade?

Each agent combines her own information with the actions she has seen. After enough agents have acted the same way, an agent may rationally ignore her private information and simply follow the crowd. Once everyone would do so, no new information enters, and learning stops: a cascade. It may be correct (the crowd likes a truthful article or dislikes a fake one) or wrong. In this model the crowd’s evidence is tracked by the public likelihood ratio ℓt, and a cascade begins when it enters an absorbing “cascade zone”.

Theory

Results at a glance

The simulations above are backed by closed-form characterisations. Here q = ½ and H is symmetric; the thresholds are those drawn in the map.

Proposition 1

Who checks

An agent with posterior belief π that the article is fake checks if and only if π lies in an interval around ½. A method that is cheap and accurate enough widens it; if \(K \ge \tfrac12(1-e_1-e_2)\), nobody checks.

\[ \pi_- = \frac{e_1+K}{1+e_1-e_2},\qquad \pi_+ = \frac{1-e_1-K}{1-e_1+e_2} \]
Learning dynamics

Cascades are absorbing

The public likelihood ratio is a Markov process. Once it enters a cascade zone, everyone likes (or everyone dislikes) without checking and it freezes. Unless checking is free and perfect in both directions, learning is incomplete.

\[ \ell_L=\frac{q(1-r)(1-e_2-K)}{(1-p)\,r\,(e_1+K)},\quad \ell_D=\frac{q(1-r)(e_2+K)}{p\,r\,(1-e_1-K)} \]
Theorem 1 & Corollary 1

Learning outcomes, by reliability

Very reliable articles are liked, very unreliable ones disliked. In between, a perfect checker still reaches the right cascade almost surely over a band of reliabilities, while an error-prone one only does so with positive probability. Thresholds of a method \(\mathcal M=(K,e_1,e_2)\):

\[ \begin{gathered} r_D=\frac{q(e_2+K)}{p(1-e_1-K)+q(e_2+K)}\\[6pt] r_L=\frac{q(1-e_2-K)}{(1-p)(e_1+K)+q(1-e_2-K)}\\[6pt] \hat r_D=\frac{q(1-e_2-K)}{p(e_1+K)+q(1-e_2-K)}\\[6pt] \hat r_L=\frac{q(e_2+K)}{(1-p)(1-e_1-K)+q(e_2+K)} \end{gathered} \]

Below rD (above rL) the crowd dislikes (likes) from the very first agent. The hatted thresholds apply to a checker that never errs, conditional on the article’s veracity.

Proposition 2 · main result

When the tool strictly lowers welfare

Let manual checking be sufficiently costly, so every agent who checks prefers the tool. Adding the tool strictly lowers welfare on the intermediate band \(\big(\hat r_L(\mathcal M_H),\hat r_D(\mathcal M_H)\big)\) whenever truthful articles are not too informative:

\[ p<\bar p=\frac{1}{1+\big(\tfrac{K_H}{1-K_H}\big)^2} \]

On either side of the band the tool can help instead. Where the sign is certified, the map above marks it with + or −.

Formal statement of Proposition 2 (regime: manual checking costly enough that only the tool is used)

Suppose H is symmetric, q = ½, and \(K_H > \frac{e_1}{1+e_1-e_2}\). Then:

  1. \(\Delta W_T=\Delta W_F=0\) for \(r\notin\big(r_D(\mathcal M_A),r_L(\mathcal M_A)\big)\).
  2. \(\Delta W_T>0\) on \(\big(r_D(\mathcal M_A),r_D(\mathcal M_H)\big)\) and \(\Delta W_T<0\) on \(\big(\hat r_L(\mathcal M_H),r_L(\mathcal M_A)\big)\).
  3. \(\Delta W_F<0\) on \(\big(r_D(\mathcal M_A),\hat r_D(\mathcal M_H)\big)\) and \(\Delta W_F>0\) on \(\big(r_L(\mathcal M_H),r_L(\mathcal M_A)\big)\).
  4. \(\Delta W<0\) on \(\big(\hat r_L(\mathcal M_H),\hat r_D(\mathcal M_H)\big)\) if \(p<\bar p\).

Here \(\Delta W=W(\{\mathcal M_H,\mathcal M_A\})-W(\{\mathcal M_H\})=r\,\Delta W_T+(1-r)\,\Delta W_F\), with \(\mathcal M_H=(K_H,0,0)\) and \(\mathcal M_A=(0,e_1,e_2)\). For the regime in which both methods are used, see Proposition 3 in the paper; the proof will appear in the journal version.

Implications

What this means for platforms

Don’t ship the tool too early

Until its accuracy passes a threshold, introducing the tool changes nothing or lowers welfare. In the paper’s simulation, welfare first falls as the tool improves from “unused” to “used by everyone who checks”, and only then rises.

An informational externality

Each agent picks her method for her own payoff. But her action also informs her successors, and a mistake of the tool can start a wrong cascade among them. Private gains and collective welfare come apart.

Even a good tool has losers

Beyond the threshold, the tool raises welfare on average, yet it still lowers it for articles of intermediate reliability when truthful articles are not too informative.

Open questions

How accurate must a tool be, analytically? Which operating point of its two error rates maximises welfare? With more than two information channels of different cost and accuracy, which should a platform offer?

Why would anyone check, and why do cascades stop checking?

An agent checks when the expected gain beats the cost. Those who are already nearly sure gain nothing, so the most ambivalent agents check. As the crowd becomes convinced, the group of checkers shrinks to nothing: that is what a cascade is.
